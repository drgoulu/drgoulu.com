It comes from the excellent YouTube channel of the math blog [3Blue1Brown](https://www.3blue1brown.com) (famous enough to have its own [Wikipedia page](w:3Blue1Brown)), and reveals something truly mind-bending:

Imagine sliding a block of mass $M=1$ toward an identical stationary block of mass $m=1$. They collide in a perfectly elastic collision (zero energy loss), the smaller block bounces against a perfectly rigid, elastic wall (second collision), travels back toward the mass $M$ block, and strikes it again (third collision).

Now, if you increase the mass of the incoming block to $M=2$, the smaller unit block ($m=1$) bounces back and forth twice between mass $M$ and the wall: totaling 5 collisions. With a cube of mass $M=8$ pushing a unit cube $m=1$ (as shown in the 3D header animation generated by the [`Jupyter notebook`](/posts/2026/collisions_pi.ipynb) below), you get 9 collisions. Intuitively, as you increase $M$, you'll get more and more bounces.

The OEIS sequence [A331859](https://oeis.org/A331859) lists the total number of collisions as a function of $M$, but doesn't go far enough to reveal THE remarkable phenomenon discovered by Gregory Galperin in 2003 [1]:

- With $M=100$, you get **31** collisions
- With $M=10,000$, you get **314** collisions
- With $M=1,000,000$, you get **3,141** collisions
- With $M=100,000,000$, you get **31,415** collisions

And before your amazed eyes, the digits of $\pi$ appear one by one: for $M=100^n$, you obtain the $n$-th prefix of $\pi$ (sequence [A011545](https://oeis.org/A011545))\*!

## But WHY???

Like you, I imagine, I saw absolutely no obvious link between this idealized mechanics thought experiment and the most famous transcendental number in mathematics.

The AI pointed me straight to the kinetic energy conservation equation:

$$ M \cdot V^2 + m \cdot v^2 = 2 E_k $$

which bears an unmistakable resemblance to the parametric equation of a circle ($x^2 + y^2 = r^2$).

By setting $x = \sqrt{M} \cdot V$, $y = \sqrt{m} \cdot v$, and $r = \sqrt{2 E_k}$, our system's velocity state $(V, v)$ traces points on a circle in the following way:

{{< figure align="aligncenter" caption="(Source: [2])" src="images/mathematics-08-00509-g004.png" >}}

Every time the small block bounces off the wall, its velocity reverses sign ($v \to -v$), which corresponds graphically to a vertical reflection across the $x$-axis. Every time the two blocks collide with each other, the state vector rotates along the circle by an angle $\theta$ such that $\tan \theta = \sqrt{m/M}$.

This process continues until the small block's velocity is too slow to catch up with the departing larger block, near the negative $x$-axis (point 9 in the diagram).

The number of collisions therefore corresponds to how many times the angle $\theta$ fits into $\pi$ radians—which, as everyone knows, is the natural [angular measure](/2010/01/16/histoire-dangles/) for a straight 180° half-turn (or rather [$\tau/2$](/2016/03/14/adieu-3-14-16-le-26-juin-ce-sera-tau-day/), but who's counting...).

Thus:

$$ \text{collisions} \approx \frac{\pi}{\theta} $$

## Where the $100^n$ Factor Comes In...

When $M \gg m$, the collision angle $\theta$ becomes exceedingly small, allowing the small-angle approximation $\tan \theta \approx \theta$.

Specifically, for $M/m = 100^n$:

$$ \theta \approx \sqrt{\frac{1}{100^n}} = \frac{1}{10^n} $$

Yielding:

$$ \text{collisions} \approx \frac{\pi}{1/10^n} = 10^n \pi $$

As the AI neatly summarized:

> It is this ultra-precise geometric mapping onto a circle that "forces" classical macroscopic mechanics to spell out the decimal digits of $\pi$ one by one.

### Note:

\* In the comments of OEIS [A011545](https://oeis.org/A011545), we find:

> Strictly speaking, this property—equivalent to claiming that the interval $(m \cdot \pi, \pi / \arctan(1/m))$ contains no integer for any $m \neq 10^n$—is not rigorously proven for all powers. In other words, it remains an open question whether A332045 could coincide with a power of 10. This is discussed in Grant Sanderson's (3Blue1Brown) follow-up video "Why colliding blocks compute pi."
