During a delightful weekend with distinguished former colleagues from the Automation Institute (Institut d'Automatique, or IA in French...), a conversation touched upon using artificial intelligence (AI, also IA in French...) in mathematics, and the level of abstraction such applications enable.

I then mentioned my brief exploration from ten years ago (already!) documented in: [Mathematical abstraction carved in stone](/2016/12/02/abstraction-mathematique-gravee-dans-le-marbre/).

Neither I nor any of my colleagues understood what the "chromatic tower of theories" discovered during that earlier post actually was, so I thought of asking an AI to popularize it:

## Explain Morava K-Theory to an Engineer

Here are the opening and concluding paragraphs of its excellent reply:

> **Morava K-theory** is an advanced tool in algebraic topology (more specifically in stable homotopy theory). For an engineer, the best mental model is an ultra-powerful mathematical spectrum analyzer designed to decompose complex geometric spaces into fundamental frequencies, called "chromatic heights."

> [(Full detailed response)](https://share.google/aimode/ChqQ6tvEsjZocgnqf)

> Morava K-theory is the ultimate tool for **algebraic spectroscopy**. It allows slicing the complex topological information of a space into independent layers (K(n)), making it possible to compute geometric invariants that would otherwise be impossible to extract from the raw signal.

All the engineers and the mathematician in the group were stunned by the marvelous sensation of realizing that a vast world existed beyond the [Fourier transform](/2010/03/06/succes-hollywoodiens-et-transformee-de-fourier/) we had used continuously throughout our careers.

At that point, Hacer, a financial analyst, asked: "What about for a five-year-old?"

I fired off a second prompt, expecting the AI to reply: "Sorry, this is too complex for a five-year-old." Instead, we received this utterly fantastic explanation:

## Explain Morava K-Theory to a 5-Year-Old

> **Imagine you have a huge bag filled with magical geometric shapes and whimsical toys. Some are simple like balloons, while others are all tangled up like balls of yarn. You'd love to tidy them up and understand how they're built, but it's really hard because they keep morphing and changing shape!**

> **Morava K-theory** is like a collection of **magical superhero glasses** to examine those toys.

> Each pair of glasses has a different color, and each color reveals a hidden secret:

> 👓 **Glasses #0 (Red):** They just show you how big the toy is. Is it huge? Does it have a big hole in the middle like a donut? That's the easiest level.

> 👓 **Glasses #1 (Orange):** They get more detailed. They show you if the toy can coil around itself like a spiral slide or a rubber band.

> 👓 **Glasses #2, 3, 4...:** The higher the number, the more powerful the glasses! They let you spot tiny, microscopic details and magical secrets hidden deep inside the toy that nobody else can see.

> If you examine your toy through **every single pair of glasses** one after another, you end up discovering absolutely all its secrets, and you know exactly how it's made!

> In short, it's a magical toolbox helping top mathematicians understand the most intricate shapes in the universe.

> ***

> Let me know if you'd like me to:
> - Use another metaphor, such as **magic sorting drawers** or **sieves for panning gold**?
> - Tell you the story of the **donut and the coffee mug** (a favorite mathematician joke)?

It is, of course, extraordinarily simplified, yet it conveys the fundamental intuition of the theory in a way anyone can grasp.

At that level, the famous theorem engraved on Urs Würgler's tombstone explains how to construct the $n$-th pair of glasses from the preceding pairs.

The discussion then naturally shifted to science communication: for a science communicator, is it more important to master the subject or to understand the target audience? The AI now gives the only possible answer: both!
