## Intro - Disclaimer

I am genuinely sorry, but we aren't going to make it. The [SMART](w:SMART_criteria) goal of the [Paris Climate Agreement](w:Paris_Agreement) to hold the increase in global average temperature well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels will not be met [[1](#references)].

Of course, we must curb our CO2 emissions as aggressively as possible, but we also have to face the fact that this won't be enough. Alas.

I also completely understand those who hammer home that there is no Plan B: if there were one, emissions reduction efforts would evaporate overnight. That is precisely why [geoengineering](w:Geoengineering) is a taboo subject. Simply bringing it up here will label me an appalling, reactionary techno-optimist accelerating the inevitable collapse of capitalist society.

Right. Yet planting trees on a massive scale is technically a form of geoengineering, already implemented widely despite its net cooling impact remaining debated.

The topic of this article is not a Plan B; it is an emergency fallback, a climate contingency plan, a last-resort measure we will very likely have to implement out of sheer desperation. Whether you like it or not...

## Dim Sun v.1

The idea proposed in 2023 in [2] is quite literally to slightly dim the Sun by generating a controlled dust cloud at the Earth-Sun [Lagrange point](w:Lagrange_point) L1.

{{< figure align="alignright" src="./images/dimsun1.png" >}}

Here is the paper's abstract:

> We explore the scenario of using dust near the Earth-Sun L1 Lagrange point as a climate mitigation measure. Our calculations account for variations in grain properties and orbital trajectories including lunar and planetary perturbations. To achieve a 1.8% reduction in solar irradiance—equivalent to roughly six days per year of sun occlusion—the required dust mass in the considered scenarios exceeds 10¹⁰ kg. Promising approaches include using highly porous, fluffy grains to boost extinction efficiency per unit mass, launched via directed streams from an L1-orbiting platform. A simpler approach involves ballistically ejecting dust grains from the lunar surface on a free trajectory toward L1, providing solar shading for several days or longer. Relative to an Earth launch, this solution offers major advantages including the immediate availability of a vast dust reservoir on the Moon and far lower kinetic energy requirements to reach the solar shield orbit.
